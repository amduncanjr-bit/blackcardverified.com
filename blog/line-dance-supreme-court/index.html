Not every line dance makes it to the canon — here's how that works

There is a process, it is unwritten, and it is not accepting applications.

Nobody held a meeting. Nobody cast a vote. And yet, the moment the first four notes of "Cha Cha Slide" drop at any function anywhere in the Western hemisphere, every able-bodied person within earshot is already moving toward the floor. That is not chaos. That is jurisprudence.

The line dance canon is real, it is binding, and it has exactly three seats on its highest court: the Electric Slide, the Cha Cha Slide, and the Wobble. Everything else is either still in appellate review or got remanded back to the regional circuit. The Cupid Shuffle has a strong case. The Dougie had its moment. The Macarena shows up like that one cousin who married in — technically present, technically tolerated. But the throne belongs to three, and it was never up for debate.

How a song earns a seat

The first requirement is that a six-year-old and a sixty-year-old have to be able to do it simultaneously without either one being embarrassed. This immediately disqualifies most of what gets submitted. The second requirement is instruction built into the lyrics. DJ Casper did not make a song — he made a tutorial with a beat. "Slide to the left" is not a lyric, it is a directive, and the fact that people still go left when he says right is a matter of personal character, not choreography. The Electric Slide predates this principle but proves it anyway: four decades, one formation, zero excuses for not knowing it.

The third requirement, and this is where most contenders wash out, is universal jurisdiction. A canonical line dance works at the cookout, at the reception, at the Juneteenth festival, at the office holiday party where you are the only one there, and somehow at the predominantly white establishment that got the DJ package off Groupon. The Wobble has cleared every single one of these venues. That alone is a constitutional achievement.

A song that tells you exactly what to do and you still do it wrong is not a flaw in the song.

Why the court stays at three

Part of what makes the canon sacred is that it is not expandable on demand. Every few years something new comes through with credentials — a rapper co-signs it, it hits at Essence Fest, somebody's auntie learns it on YouTube — and the culture watches to see if it sticks. Most things don't. Not because they weren't good, but because canonization requires time, and time requires surviving multiple generations of DJs with different opinions about what the floor needs at 10:47 PM.

The Electric Slide has been doing this since 1989. It has outlasted several music formats, at least three distinct eras of R&B beef, and every prediction that line dances were over. It is not over. It will never be over. When they play "Electric Boogie" at an event, people form a line with the muscle memory of something ancestral, and nobody questions why they know this. They just know.

That is the thing about the canon. It doesn't ask for your buy-in. It already has it. You proved it the last time you were on a floor and heard that opening, felt your feet move before your brain caught up, and found yourself in formation next to someone you've never met, both of you doing the exact same thing at the exact same time. That moment of synchronized recognition — that's not a line dance. That's a shared text.

The court is not accepting new cases. Step to the left.
