Everything you need to know about HBCU homecoming and nothing that will actually prepare you

A survival guide for an experience that cannot be survived, only lived.

You have been told about homecoming. You have seen the pictures, watched the highlight reels, heard the stories that start with "so we were at Spelhouse" or "this one Aggie Fest" or "Howard '09, you wouldn't understand." You think you are ready. You are not ready. Nobody has ever been ready. That is the whole point.

HBCU homecoming is not an event. It is a convergence — of alumni who have not seen each other since graduation and act like it's been forty years, of students who are currently in the process of making memories they will describe inaccurately for the rest of their lives, and of that one person who went to a PWI and shows up anyway, standing slightly to the left of everything, trying to figure out why the energy is different. The energy is different because the energy has always been different. That's not something you can explain. It's something you absorb.

The schedule is a suggestion and also the law

Homecoming week has an official schedule. There are step shows and yard parties and coronations and tailgates and concerts and greek showcases and alumni brunches. You will attend approximately forty percent of what you planned and one hundred percent of what you didn't. Someone will text you at 11 PM about something happening in a parking lot and that will be the best part of the whole weekend. Write that down.

The yard during homecoming is its own jurisdiction. The fashion alone is a semester's worth of study — people come dressed like they are receiving an honorary degree and also like they are ready to run if necessary. Both are correct. Presentation matters at homecoming in a way that is deeply serious and also completely joyful, which is a combination that HBCUs have always understood and the rest of the world keeps trying to figure out.

You will attend forty percent of what you planned and one hundred percent of what you didn't — and the parking lot at 11 PM will win every time.

The band is not background music

If you have never stood in the stands when an HBCU marching band enters the field, there is a gap in your education that no syllabus has addressed. These are not people playing instruments while something else happens. The band is the thing that is happening. The arrangements, the formations, the battle of the bands where every school's section is personally offended by the other school's section — this is a whole art form with its own history, its own legends, its own moments that people remember exactly where they were for. Treat it accordingly.

The Greek shows will also require your full attention and a working knowledge of the fact that every organization believes, sincerely, that they did it best. They are all correct within their own jurisdiction. Do not adjudicate this out loud.

What you actually take home

Here is what homecoming is really about, underneath all of it: it is proof. Proof that these institutions — built by and for Black people at a time when the alternative was nothing — produced something that cannot be quantified on a ranking list. The community that forms at an HBCU is not incidental to the education. It is the education. The confidence you see on that yard, the way people carry themselves, the way alumni come back decade after decade like they're answering a call — that's the curriculum.

You can test your HBCU knowledge on Black Card Verified, but homecoming itself is a category with no wrong answers, only experiences you haven't had yet. Go. Find your people. Lose the itinerary. The parking lot will find you.
