Cookout assignments, ranked

Nobody asked for your input on where you were placed and nobody wants to hear it now.

Every cookout runs on an invisible org chart. Auntie is the CEO. The person whose house it is? Facilities management. And the rest of us have been assigned roles we did not apply for, cannot appeal, and will hold until further notice. The assignment reflects your standing. It reflects your history. It reflects what somebody's mama said about you one time in 2014. Here is the definitive ranking, from the bottom to the throne.

The bottom tier is a whole spiritual experience

Ice duty is dead last and it's not close. You were handed a twenty and a grocery list and sent to three different stores because the first one only had cubed and somebody's uncle specifically requested crushed. You missed the potato salad coming out. You missed the first round of plates. You arrived back sweating, arms full, and someone said "oh good, put it in the cooler" without looking up. Ice duty is assigned to people who showed up too eager or too late — the cookout does not reward either.

Just above ice is watching the kids, which sounds like a light assignment until you realize there are eleven of them, the oldest is nine, and they have collectively decided that today is the day they test every boundary ever established. You are not their parent. You have no real authority. You are simply a obstacle they are choosing, in real time, to respect or not respect. Good luck.

Drink runs and chair setup live in the middle — respectable grunt work, nothing more. You did something. The cookout acknowledges you. Move on.

The grill is not a promotion. The grill is a presidency. You are accountable for everything and everyone will have notes.

The upper tier is where legacies are made

Let's talk about the grill. This is not a casual assignment. Nobody just ends up at the grill. You were vetted over years of attendance, your previous plate quality was discussed in rooms you were not in, and someone — a specific elder — decided you were ready. The grill man (and it is usually, though not always, a man, which is its own conversation) holds the schedule for the entire day. The hot links go when he says they go. The chicken comes off when he decides it is time. If he is wrong, everyone suffers. If he is right, he will mention it at the next four cookouts.

Potato salad is the wildcard tier. Bringing the potato salad means either everything or nothing. If it's good, you are a pillar of the community. If it tastes off — too much mustard, not enough egg, some kind of situation with the relish — you will be talked about respectfully and never asked again. There is no try. There is only the potato salad.

And then there is the mac and cheese chair. You did not bring the mac and cheese. You did not cook the mac and cheese. But you are sitting closest to it, you have the serving spoon, and everyone has to come to you. This is pure social power. You regulate portion sizes. You decide who gets the corner piece. You are unbothered, comfortable, and entirely in control of the most important dish on the table. The mac and cheese chair is not given. It is assumed by someone who understood the assignment before the assignment was ever explained.

Know your role, own your rank

Here is the truth about cookout assignments: complaining about yours only gets you reassigned to something worse. You take ice duty with grace, you work your way up, and one day — maybe years from now — you will find yourself in a lawn chair, serving spoon in hand, and you will know you have arrived.
